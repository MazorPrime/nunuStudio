Why source code is the wrong lever

Kenshi's code isn't public and isn't licensed for derivative works. Even if you obtained it, you couldn't legally ship anything built from it. And it's a ~12-year-old custom C++ codebase built on OGRE, largely written by one person — Kenshi's development was primarily led by a single person over twelve years. It's known for being single-threaded and heavily bottlenecked on pathfinding. Inheriting it means inheriting all of that. 
Wikipedia

What you probably actually want: the FCS

Kenshi ships with the Forgotten Construction Set, which exposes nearly all game data — factions, items, squads, dialogue, world map, towns, AI packages, stats, races, research. Total conversions like Genesis and Universal Wasteland Expansion are effectively different games built entirely through it. If your goal is "Kenshi but with my mechanics/setting/balance," this is a weekend-to-months project depending on scope, it's legal, it's supported, and there's a mod community to lean on. The limit is that you can't change core engine behavior — combat tick math, pathfinding, rendering.

If you mean building your own from scratch

That's the genuinely hard version. The brutal parts aren't the RPG systems, they're:

Dynamic navmesh — player-built bases must re-path in real time. This is the single nastiest problem in the genre.
Scale simulation — hundreds of NPCs with squad AI, faction relations, and a running economy, off-screen, without melting the CPU.
Melee combat with N participants — animation blending, per-limb damage, KO/bleed/recovery states.
Content volume — Kenshi's world is ~870 km² with dozens of factions. Systems are maybe 30% of the work; content is the rest.
In Unreal or Unity today you'd skip rendering, physics, and asset pipeline entirely, which is a huge head start. Realistically: 3–6 experienced people, 2–4 years for a stripped-down version. Solo and self-taught, you're looking at 5+ years and a high odds of not finishing — which is the normal outcome, not a knock on you.

The honest middle path

Build a small, ugly prototype of the one mechanic you actually care about — squad combat, or base-building pathing, or the faction sim — in Godot or Unity, top-down, capsules for characters. Two months. If that core loop is fun with programmer art, you have something. If it isn't, no amount of Kenshi source code would have saved it.

Your intuition is right, and it has a name: data-driven architecture. The foundation isn't the content — it's an engine that reads content from data files. RimWorld's entire game is defined in XML "defs": every item, plant, animal, trait, body part, and recipe is a data entry, not code. Kenshi's FCS is the same idea with a GUI. Once that layer exists, adding a new weapon or faction or disease is filling in a row, not writing a system. That's why both games grew for years and why their mod scenes are so deep.

On "hardest to code" — I'd push back. They're not, technically. A modern AAA renderer or rollback netcode for a fighting game is far harder engineering. What's hard about Kenshi and RimWorld is systems design: getting a dozen simple rules to collide in ways that generate stories. That's tuning and taste, not algorithms. The good news is that's learnable by iteration. The bad news is you can't shortcut it — you find it by playing your own broken prototype 200 times.

RimWorld is a much better target than Kenshi for you. Tile grid instead of 3D navmesh, 2D sprites instead of animated 3D characters and rigs, no melee animation blending, no terrain streaming.
